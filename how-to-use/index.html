How to use

Backstory

You saw something in the news, on social media, or in pop culture and want to know what's really behind it. Backstory finds the history and ideas underneath it and gives you a ranked list of real books, articles, and journals to read next.

1. Bring it in

At the top of the page, choose how you want to bring in what you're curious about:

Type a topicA few words, a name, an event, or a question. "Mining massacre in Central Africa" or "Why does everyone keep quoting 1984?"
Paste textCopy the article, post, or passage and paste it in. Best for long news stories.
Upload a fileA PDF, Word (.docx), or text file. It's read in your browser and never uploaded.
Paste a linkMost news sites don't let other web pages read them, so Backstory reads the words in the link's address (a lot of news links contain the headline). Wikipedia links are read in full. If the results look thin, paste the article's text instead.

Add a focus if you want one, like who profits, what the law says, or how it started. Readings that speak to your focus move up the list.

Pick a folder to save the topic in, then press Find the backstory.

2. Check the search words

Backstory picks search words from what you brought in. From pasted text it looks for names, places, and phrases that repeat. You can see every word it will search. Remove any that are off (press ×) and add your own.

Good search words are names of things: events, places, ideas, laws, people. "Kimberley Process" works better than "diamond rules." Two to four words is usually best.

3. Read the backstory in brief

Section 2 shows the idea underneath it: a short summary from Wikipedia, quoted word for word with a link. Wikipedia is a good first stop, not the last one. Check the sources at the bottom of the full article.

Below it, angles in your story shows which kinds of history help explain it: history, politics and power, economics and trade, ethics and human rights, law and policy, society and media, science and environment. The number is Jev's yes-chance for each one. The darker chips are the angles over 50%.

4. Read the reading list

Section 3 starts with a short reading path:

  • Start here. Readings written for general readers.
  • Go deeper. Academic readings for after you know the basics.
  • From every angle. One reading for each angle in your story, so you don't only hear one side of it.

Then the full list, ranked. Use the buttons above it to show only books, only articles, only free readings, or one angle. Each reading looks like this:

An example of how a reading looks. The numbers are made up for this guide.

TagsWhat kind of reading it is, its main angle, its reading level (Intro, Accessible, Academic, Specialist), and three flags: One side (it argues a position, so read it next to something from another angle), Firsthand (journalism, memoir, or testimony), and Free.
The "why" lineWritten by rules from Jev's numbers and the catalog. "2.7 of 3" is how related Jev thinks it is.
Where to get itOpen-access readings link straight to a free copy. Open Library books can sometimes be borrowed free online with an Open Library account. Everything else links to WorldCat (find it in a library near you) or the publisher's page through its DOI. Your campus library can usually get a DOI article for you.
What the catalog saysThe abstract, first sentence, or subjects, straight from the catalog.
Jev's numbersEvery question Jev was asked about this reading and every answer it gave.

At the bottom, Probably off-topic holds readings Jev scored as unrelated. They're hidden, not deleted, in case Jev got one wrong.

5. Ask the guide

The little librarian in the bottom-right corner is the guide. A red dot means it has something for you. Click it for the short version:

  • what your topic is about, in one sentence,
  • why it's bigger than one story,
  • what to read if you have 10 minutes, an evening, or a few weeks,
  • and one thing to keep in mind, like an angle that's missing from the list.

The guide doesn't make anything up. Each line is filled in from the catalogs and Jev's numbers. Press Esc or × to close it.

6. Folders and your library

Folders (on the left, or under the Folders button on a phone) keep your topics organized, like Labor history or Things from TikTok. Every search is saved in the folder you picked. Click a topic to see its list again. Use ⋯ to rename or delete a folder.

Press Save to library on any reading to keep it. My library (at the top) sorts your saved readings into To read, Reading, and Finished. Each one has a note box for what it taught you. Copy reading list copies everything, with links and notes, so you can paste it into a document.

Your folders, library, and notes are saved in this browser on this device only. Backstory has no accounts and collects nothing. Clearing your browser's data erases them, so copy your reading list now and then.

7. Go deeper with Gemini

Nothing in sections 1 to 3 is written by a chatbot. If you want the ideas explained in more depth, section 4 builds a Backstory packet: your topic, the background, and your ranked reading list with links. Take it to Gemini with the Backstory Guide skill:

  1. Once: make a Gem. In Gemini, open Gems → New Gem, name it Backstory Guide, paste the Gem instructions into Instructions, and upload SKILL.md as knowledge. (Both are under See the skill.)
  2. Each time: press Copy packet and paste it into the Gem.

No Gem? Copy skill + packet and paste both into a new chat.

The skill tells Gemini to explain in plain language, cover every angle, mark what's well established versus debated, and recommend only readings from your list. It can offer moves like Timeline, Key terms, Who's who, Every angle, Reading plan, Claim check, and Then and now. Even so, check anything important against the readings. That's what they're for.

8. How it works

Layer 1: the catalogsWikipedia for background. Open Library for books. OpenAlex for journal articles and scholarly books, with Crossref as a backup. DOAJ for open-access articles. All free and open.
Layer 2: JevJev is an AI model from TypeSafe AI that answers questions with numbers instead of sentences. For each reading, Backstory asks: how related is it, what's its main angle, who is it written for, does it argue one side, is it firsthand, and does it speak to your focus? Rules in the app turn those numbers into the ranking, the tags, and the "why" lines. Turn off Rank with Jev to see the catalogs' own order.
Layer 3: GeminiOnly if you choose to paste a packet. Backstory never sends anything to Gemini.

What leaves your browser: your search words go to the catalogs. Short catalog entries, plus the start of what you brought in, go to Jev through a proxy that stores nothing. Uploaded files, folders, and your library stay on your device.

9. Tips and limits

  • Thin results? Use fewer, more specific search words, or add the name of the bigger idea ("conflict diamonds", not "mine attack").
  • Missing an angle? The list tells you. Add a search word for that angle, like "law" or "labor", and search again.
  • Very recent news may not have books or articles yet. The background will still help, and the older history usually matters most.
  • Jev can be wrong. It reads only titles, subjects, and abstracts, not whole books. Open "Jev's numbers" if a ranking surprises you.
  • Catalogs can be messy. Some entries are in other languages, are study guides, or have odd titles. Skip those.
  • OpenAlex has a daily limit for apps without a key. If it's busy, Backstory uses Crossref, and the list says so.
